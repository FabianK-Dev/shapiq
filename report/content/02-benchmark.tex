\section{Setup}
\label{sec:benchmark:setup}

\begin{configtable}{Benchmark configuration}{Benchmark configuration. Provenance:
    \texttt{benchmark/results/lmu\_full\_sweep\_20260717/README.md}.}
    Games & 11 --- SOUM($n$=6,8,10) and the ML games IRIS(4), California(8), Diabetes(10),
        Adult(12), Correlated(60), Independent(60), NHANES(79), Communities(101), following
        the grid of \citet{Witter.2025a}. \\
    Value function & Single baseline row (training mean); \texttt{XGBRegressor}, 100 trees,
        depth 4. \emph{Not} any paper's --- see Table~\ref{tab:benchmark:vfs}. \\
    Ground truth & Exact enumeration for $n \le 10$; interventional TreeSHAP against the
        same baseline row above it. \\
    Budgets & $m = c \cdot n$, $c \in \{1,2,3,5,8,12,20,30,50,75,110,160\}$. \\
    Repeats & 10 held-out predictions per game, model fixed. Median and IQR over them. \\
    Methods & All of \texttt{shapiq.approximator.SV\_APPROXIMATORS} (15 registered, 14 ran). \\
    Scale & 19{,}800 cells, 143{,}980 metric rows, LMU CIP cluster. \\
\end{configtable}

Budgets are multiples of $n$, not fractions of $2^n$. A fraction of the coalition space stops
being runnable as $n$ grows --- five percent of Independent($n$=60) is $5.8 \times 10^{16}$
evaluations --- and a budget of $2^n$ makes every method exact, so ranks there record ties. A
repeat explains one prediction of one \emph{fixed} model: had it retrained, the band would mix
estimator noise with model noise, since a different tree has different Shapley values.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:benchmark:results}

Averaged over all eleven games \leverageshap{} ranks best at $3.24$ and \oddshap{} follows at
$3.31$ --- yet \oddshap{} wins outright $652$ times to \leverageshap{}'s $168$. A method cannot come
first four times as often and still be worse on average, unless the average is carried by cells
where coming first means little. Table~\ref{tab:benchmark:regimes} splits by dimension and the
ordering inverts.

The inversion is saturation, not noise. A quarter of small-game results are already exact ---
Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark:soum} shows every method collapsing to the floor as $m \to 2^{10}$ ---
against one percent of large-game results. Where a quarter of the cells are ties, a rank is a
tie-break. The large games carry the comparison.

\subsection*{Each method against its own paper}

The two regimes are a division of labour, not a ranking, and each half is what the method's own
paper predicts (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark:claims}).

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption[Each paper's claim against our result]{Each paper's own claim, tested here. All
        three hold, on a value function none of them uses.}
    \label{tab:benchmark:claims}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.16\textwidth}p{0.44\textwidth}p{0.32\textwidth}@{}}
        \toprule
        Method & Its paper's claim & Our result \\
        \midrule
        \polyshap{} & Best at low dimension; higher order ``requires a larger sampling
            budget'' \citep{Fumagalli.2026a} & 2nd small, 6th large --- the largest swing \\
        \leverageshap{} & Beats optimized KernelSHAP, ``especially for large $n$''
            \citep{Witter.2025a} & Leads it by $1.20$ small, $1.89$ large \\
        \oddshap{} & Clearly outperforms flexible-budget baselines once $m > \eta d$
            \citep{Fumagalli.2026} & 1st large; rank $\approx 1.0$ from $12d$, $\eta = 10$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\polyshap{} swings furthest, and its paper states the mechanism: the support is every interaction
up to a fixed order $k$, costing $O(d^k)$ terms. At $d = 101$ that is unaffordable at any budget
in our grid, so the method degrades to the field. Its regime is real and narrow.

\leverageshap{} is the uniform one, never far from the top. Its claim is narrower than a
leaderboard position --- it names a specific opponent, which is in our grid --- and holds with the
qualifier intact: the lead over OptimizedKernelSHAP \emph{grows} from $1.20$ to $1.89$ ranks with
dimension, as an $O(n \log n)$ guarantee against a heuristic should.

\oddshap{} is \polyshap{}'s mirror image, and its advantage switches on at a predicted budget:
rank $3.40$ at $m = d$, then $1.35$ at $2d$, $1.30$ at $5d$, and $1.00$–$1.07$ throughout
$12d$–$160d$. With $\eta = 10$ that threshold is $m > \eta d$, the condition
\citet{Fumagalli.2026} attaches to its own claim. Below it the screening cannot pay for itself;
above it an adaptive support beats \polyshap{}'s fixed one, which is why the two sit at opposite
ends of the dimension axis. The runner-up above the threshold is always \leverageshap{}, drifting
from $2.05$ at $12d$ to $2.65$ at $160d$: the gap widens with budget rather than closing, as it
should if the two differ in what they can represent rather than in how fast they converge.

\subsection*{What this does and does not license}

Each paper defines its own value function --- \citet{Fumagalli.2026} XGBoost classifiers perturbed
interventionally over fifty background instances, \citet{Fumagalli.2026a} random forests perturbed
path-dependently, \citet{Witter.2025a} tree models whose exact values TreeSHAP supplies --- and
this benchmark uses a fourth (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark:vfs}). So nothing here reproduces or refutes
anything. What it offers is a cross-check: three claims, each made under its own conditions, each
still visible under conditions belonging to nobody. That licenses less than a reproduction, since
it cannot confirm a number, and suggests more, since a regime surviving a change of value function
is likelier to be a property of the method than of the setup. Only the orderings transfer, not the
magnitudes.

\subsection*{What the error metric hides}

The orderings survive a change of \emph{error} metric --- under the relative $\ell_2$ error that
\citet{Witter.2025a} reports, \leverageshap{} still leads the small games ($3.64$ to \polyshap{}'s
$3.77$) and \oddshap{} still the large ($1.71$ to $3.22$). They do not survive a change of
\emph{question}. Table~\ref{tab:benchmark:metrics} reranks the large games by how well each method
recovers the feature \emph{ordering} rather than the values, and the result is close to an
inversion.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption[Rank under three metrics, and cost, large games]{Mean rank on the large games under
        squared error, top-5 precision and rank correlation, with the median cost of one estimate.
        The three approximators added here are in bold. Each wins on magnitude and loses on
        ordering; two plain samplers do the reverse, at a fraction of the cost.}
    \label{tab:benchmark:metrics}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lcccr@{}}
        \toprule
        Method & MSE & Precision@5 & Kendall's $\tau$ & Cost (s) \\
        \midrule
        \textbf{\oddshap{}}        & \textbf{1.34} & 6.67           & 4.68          & 0.171 \\
        \textbf{\leverageshap{}}   & 2.69          & \textbf{4.28}  & 6.48          & 0.016 \\
        \textbf{\polyshap{}}       & 5.94          & 10.04          & 7.47          & 0.720 \\
        \midrule
        PermutationSamplingSV      & 5.91          & 6.23           & \textbf{1.85} & \textbf{0.008} \\
        OwenSamplingSV             & 7.72          & 5.23           & 2.71          & 0.266 \\
        RegressionMSR              & 5.17          & 7.27           & 6.87          & 0.124 \\
        OptimizedKernelSHAP        & 4.58          & 9.46           & 8.15          & 0.041 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The pattern is a family trait, not a quirk of one method. All three approximators added here are
regression estimators fitting a least-squares objective --- which is what MSE rewards --- and all
three lose the ordering metrics to samplers that estimate each player's marginal contribution
directly. Permutation and Owen sampling take the top two places on Kendall's $\tau$ while sitting
sixth and ninth on MSE; \oddshap{} inverts it exactly, first on MSE and sixth on Precision@5.
Minimising squared error over a sparse support recovers the large coefficients and leaves the
near-ties among the small ones in the residual, where MSE cannot see them and an ordering cannot
survive them. Since a Shapley value is usually read as a ranking of features rather than a vector
of magnitudes, this is the sharpest "where do they lose" the benchmark has to offer --- and none of
the three papers reports it, because all three evaluate on error, as does the rest of this chapter.

Cost splits the three the same way and answers the obvious objection to \oddshap{}: its screening
step fits a gradient-boosted tree, so one might expect its accuracy to be bought with time. It is,
but the price is modest --- $0.171$\,s against \leverageshap{}'s $0.016$\,s, both far below SPEX's
$3.2$\,s or ProxySPEX's $6.3$\,s. The three trade off cleanly rather than dominating one another:
\leverageshap{} is the cheapest of them and second on error; \oddshap{} pays eleven times that for
first place; \polyshap{} pays forty-five times and still finishes sixth at these dimensions, which
is the same fixed-order support that costs it the large games showing up on the clock as well.

One deviation is worth stating rather than smoothing. \citet{Fumagalli.2026} reports that at low
dimension \oddshap{} performs \emph{like} RegressionMSR; here it is ahead, $4.51$ against $5.62$.
Our small games are smaller than the paper's low-dimensional value functions ($n \le 12$ against
$d = 14$–$16$) and the value function differs, so the two are not in contradiction --- but they are
not the same statement either.

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
Five, in descending order of how much they would change a conclusion.

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.15em}
    \item \textbf{Uneven coverage.} Average ranks across methods that ran in different numbers of
        cells are not directly comparable. \oddshap{} declines 50 --- all at $m = d$ or $2d$ on the
        four smallest games, below its minimum of $\eta = 10$ --- and none of the 480 large ones.
        SPEX declines 680, ProxySPEX 10.
    \item \textbf{Where the inversion happens is untested.} No game sits between $n = 12$ and
        $n = 60$, so the small/large split is a gap in the grid, not a measured threshold.
    \item \textbf{Coarse bands.} Ten predictions make the quartiles an envelope, not a confidence
        interval.
    \item \textbf{An incomplete baseline set.} FourierSHAP and the fixed-budget FFD variants are in
        \citet{Fumagalli.2026}'s comparison but absent from \shapiq{}, so the baselines here are
        that paper's set intersected with what exists.
    \item \textbf{One method never ran.} ShaplEIG is registered but needs an uninstalled extra, and
        is skipped in all $1{,}320$ of its cells.
\end{itemize}
